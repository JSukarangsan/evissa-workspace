## Key Concepts
- Citizen developers aren't a risk to manage — they're a cultural phenomenon happening right now inside companies adopting AI. Grassroots, bottom-up, people building things because the tools finally let them
- Companies are obsessed with lanes. Job descriptions, swim lanes, RACI charts. Management loves lanes because lanes make management easier. But the companies that will survive AI aren't the ones with the cleanest org chart — they're the ones who let people break out of lanes
- If you're not a native tech company, citizen developers are your technological advantage. You don't have a 200-person eng team. You have a marketing ops person who built an AI workflow that saves 40 hours a week. That's your asset. Cultivate it
- The governance question is real but secondary. Most companies lead with governance (CoE tiers, graduation criteria, compliance frameworks) because it feels safe. But governance without cultivation kills the movement before it starts
- The right framing: foster first, govern second. Build the cultural permission for people to build, then layer in guardrails. Not the reverse

## Content Angles
- Angle 1: *The grassroots movement companies keep trying to squash.* Citizen developers are emerging organically in every company using AI tools. Most companies respond with governance frameworks. The smart ones respond with investment
- Angle 2: *Lanes are a management convenience, not a strategy.* The org chart exists to make management legible. It was never designed to maximize what people can contribute. AI is exposing that gap
- Angle 3: *Your non-tech company's unfair advantage.* If you're a $50M services company and your account manager built an AI tool that automates client reporting — that person is more strategically valuable than most of your IT department. Act accordingly
- Angle 4: *Governance that doesn't kill the movement.* Practical take — yes you need tiers, graduation criteria, CoE structure. But sequence matters. Permission → experimentation → patterns → governance. Not governance → permission

## Visual Assets (from Notion)

Jon has a detailed infographic spec for governance models. Sketch/hand-drawn aesthetic, black pen on paper, minimal color, max info density. Could be LinkedIn carousel.

### Visual 1: Center of Excellence Structure
Hub-and-spoke diagram. Center = CoE (Govern, Train, Monitor, Enable). Spokes to departments. Inward arrows: Requests, Questions, App Submissions. Outward: Training, Guardrails, Approved Platforms, Support. Key callout: "IT provides platforms, business retains ownership" / "Enabler, not gatekeeper."

### Visual 2: Environment Tiers (Pyramid)
- Top (most governance): Enterprise Products — full security review, SLAs, change management
- Middle-high: Department Solutions — dept head sign-off, IT security review, audits
- Middle-low: Team Tools — manager approval, basic security checklist, quarterly review
- Bottom (least governance): Personal Productivity — self-service, sandboxed, no production data

### Visual 3: Graduation Criteria Checklist
"When does Sarah's tool become company product?" — 10-item checklist: security review, documentation, support owner, data governance, performance tested, backup/recovery, user training, monitoring, IT sign-off, maintenance budget

### Visual 4: Product Engineer Spectrum
Horizontal spectrum from Software Engineer (owns code, deep specialization) → Product Engineer / New Builder (owns product AND build, full-stack accountability) → Product Manager (owns outcomes, broad context)
