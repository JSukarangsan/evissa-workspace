## Core Insight

The standardization instinct in enterprise AI fails for a reason most leaders haven't diagnosed correctly. It's not that different roles need different tools (though they do). It's that the same person, in the same role, uses different tools for different cognitive tasks — and is at different proficiency levels across all of them.

A designer isn't a designer. They're someone who uses Figma for structure, Midjourney for inspiration, v0 for rapid prototyping, and Claude for critique loops. They're a power user in one, a complete beginner in another, and actively avoiding a third because it doesn't fit how they think. Now multiply that by 20 designers on a team. You don't have a tool proliferation problem. You have 20 people working out, independently, what actually fits their workflow.

Shadow IT isn't a compliance failure. It's the org telling you something true: the authorized tooling doesn't map to the actual texture of the work.

## The Reframe

Most AI adoption programs are designed around a fantasy: that roles are homogeneous, that all designers need the same thing, that one training program will work for everyone at the same level.

The proficiency reality from the NYT engagement breaks this down fast. In any given team, roughly:
- **Cautious (50-65%):** Not resistant. Waiting. They haven't found the fit yet — usually because they've been handed a tool that solves someone else's problem.
- **Motivated (25-35%):** Want to use AI more. Blocked by the wrong tool, wrong context, or wrong starting point for their current skill level.
- **Engaged (5-15%):** Already using multiple tools, already building workflows, already frustrated that the official stack doesn't include what's actually working.

The Engaged cohort has already solved the optionality problem for themselves. The Cautious cohort needs a different path in than the one the Engaged cohort took. One policy cannot serve both.

## Key Concepts

- **Within-role diversity is the real problem.** Cross-role differences (PM vs. designer vs. engineer) are easy to see. Within-role differences — same title, same team, different workflows, different proficiency levels — are invisible until you look for them.
- **Tool fit is task-specific, not role-specific.** The right question isn't "what's the right AI tool for designers?" It's "what's the right tool for this specific design task at this person's current proficiency level?"
- **Proficiency is multi-dimensional.** Someone can be an advanced Figma user and a complete beginner with generative tools. Their adoption journey looks nothing like a colleague who's the inverse.
- **Optionality isn't chaos — it's honest policy.** A hub-and-spoke Center of Practice (not a CoE) can hold multiple pathways with guardrails, without the bureaucracy that kills adoption momentum.
- **Shadow IT is signal, not noise.** When people use unauthorized tools, they're telling you the authorized ones don't fit. The diagnostic question is: what does the unauthorized tool do that the authorized one doesn't?

## Content Angles

**The designer example (most concrete, most shareable):**
Walk through a single designer's actual AI workflow across a day. Four tools, four different cognitive tasks, three different proficiency levels. This is the unit of analysis adoption programs miss. It's not "designers." It's this person, this task, this level.

**The standardization trap (most counterintuitive):**
Orgs that enforce single-tool policies don't reduce tool proliferation. They reduce visible tool proliferation. The real workflows go underground. You've created shadow IT by trying to prevent it.

**The proficiency tier model (most practical):**
The three-cohort breakdown is the governance answer. Each tier needs a different intervention:
- Cautious: reduce friction, find the specific task where AI fits their actual work
- Motivated: give them better tools, not more training on the wrong one
- Engaged: get out of their way, and let them document what's working so it spreads

**The hub-and-spoke model (most actionable for leaders):**
Not a 10-person CoE. One person or team who holds the map of what's working across the org, enables multiple pathways, and removes blockers. Governance as enablement rather than control.

## Frameworks

- **Shadow IT as Signal Framework:** Reframes unauthorized tool use from compliance problem to adoption diagnostic
